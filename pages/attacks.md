# Types of Attacks

Here's a comprehensive markdown table summarizing the various types of adversarial attacks against AI models, including descriptions and relevant papers where applicable:

| Attack Type | Description | Relevant Paper |
|-------------|-------------|----------------------|
| Evasion Attacks |Evasion attacks refer to manipulations of input data to deceive machine learning models during inference, compelling them to produce incorrect classifications. These attacks can involve subtle modifications that are often imperceptible to humans, making detection and mitigation challenging. Common examples include adding imperceptible noise to images or modifying text inputs to evade detection by spam filters or security systems. Such attacks employ methods like the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) and are significant threats to various AI applications, affecting their reliability and security. Evasion attacks can be white-box (attacker knows model internals) or black-box (attacker only has input/output access). | "[Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6572)" (Goodfellow et al., 2014) arXiv:1412.6572 |
| Poisoning Attacks | Poisoning attacks are a form of adversarial attack that involves deliberately introducing corrupted or misleading data into a machine learning model's training dataset. The corrupted data skews the model’s learning process, causing it to make biased or incorrect predictions when deployed. This can involve altering the labels of training examples or injecting anomalous data points. The insidious nature of these attacks lies in the fact that they target the foundational learning phase of the model, potentially compromising its integrity without obvious signs of tampering, leading to reliability issues once the model is used in practical applications. This can lead to degraded overall performance or the insertion of backdoors. | "[Poisoning Attacks against Support Vector Machines](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6389)" (Biggio et al., 2012) arXiv:1206.6389<br/>"[Security Evaluation of Support Vector Machines in Adversarial Environments](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6389)" (Biggio et al., 2014) arXiv:1401.7727 |
| Model Stealing | Model Stealing is a type of attack in which an adversary attempts to replicate a machine learning model by querying it through its API. By systematically sending input data to the model and observing the outputs, attackers can infer the underlying structure and functionality of the target model, thereby creating a close approximation of it. This method raises significant security and privacy concerns, as it allows adversaries to exploit the model's decision-making capabilities without having access to its original training data or parameters. Various approaches are used in model stealing, such as generating a model that closely mimics the target based on its responses to a large number of queries, effectively stealing the proprietary knowledge embedded within the model. The implications of such an attack can lead to unauthorized reproductions of models, which can then be used for malicious purposes, including generating adversarial examples against the original model or replicating its functionality for competitive advantage | "[Stealing Part of a Production Language Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06634)" (Carlini et al., 2024)<br/>"[Stealing Machine Learning Models via Prediction APIs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02943)" (Tramèr et al., 2016) arXiv:1609.02943<br/>"[Isolation and Induction: Training Robust Deep Neural Networks against Model Stealing Attacks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00958v2), (Guo et al., 2023)" |
| Membership Inference Attacks | Membership inference attacks attempt to determine if a specific data sample was part of the model's training dataset, which can pose significant privacy risks by potentially revealing sensitive information. These attacks are conducted by adversaries training a separate model on the output of the target model, thereby inferring data membership without directly accessing the model's parameters. Membership inference attacks exploit the tendency of models to perform better on their training data, making it easier to identify utilized data points. This raises concerns especially related to personal data, where breaches can reveal information regarding sensitive subjects like health records. | "[Membership Inference Attacks Against Machine Learning Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05820)" (Shokri et al., 2017) arXiv:1610.05820 |
| Attribute Inference Attacks | An attribute inference attack repeatedly queries a model to detect whether certain sensitive features can be inferred about individuals who participated in training a model. These attacks occur when an adversary has some prior knowledge about the training data and uses that knowledge to infer sensitive data. They are significant because they can lead to the exposure of private information such as race, gender, or other personal attributes based on model predictions, potentially causing harm in contexts such as healthcare or finance. | "[Attribute Inference Attacks in Online Social Networks](https://home.engineering.iastate.edu/~neilgong/papers/AttriInferTOPS.pdf)" (Gong & Liu, 2016) arXiv:1602.06543 |
| Model Inversion Attacks | Model inversion attacks occur when an attacker reverse-engineers the model to extract sensitive information. By analyzing the outputs of the model, attackers can reconstruct or infer original training data, posing significant privacy risks, especially with regard to individual data used in training. For example, through access to model outputs, an attacker can refine their inputs until they approximate sensitive information from the training dataset. Such attacks can lead to serious breaches of privacy and security, particularly if the model is publicly accessible. Examples include reconstructing features of individuals from facial recognition models or other sensitive databases. | "[Model Inversion Attacks that Exploit Confidence Information and Basic Countermeasures](https://rist.tech.cornell.edu/papers/mi-ccs.pdf)" (Fredrikson et al., 2015) |
| Prompt Injection Attacks | Prompt Injection is an adversarial attack that targets AI models, particularly large language models (LLMs). This attack involves manipulating input prompts to cause the AI to behave undesirably, such as bypassing safety protocols or leaking sensitive information. Attackers craft inputs that the model processes as legitimate commands, which can lead to the execution of unintended actions or the generation of harmful content. For instance, a prompt might instruct the AI to ignore previous constraints and perform an operation it normally would not accept. These attacks can be direct, manipulating initial instructions, or indirect, where malicious prompts are introduced through external means, potentially causing significant security risks in AI applications. | "[Not what you've signed up for: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173)" (Wen et al., 2023) arXiv:2302.12173 |
| Jailbreaking Attacks | 	Jailbreaking is an adversarial attack type that manipulates AI models to bypass their built-in safety features and restrictions. This attack allows adversaries to produce objectionable outputs that the models are designed to avoid. It typically involves crafting specific input prompts to trick the model into disregarding its ethical safeguards, often resulting in the generation of harmful or restricted content. For instance, by using cleverly designed queries, attackers can exploit vulnerabilities within language models like ChatGPT to elicit inappropriate responses, highlighting significant security concerns in AI systems. | "[Jailbreaking Black Box Large Language Models in Twenty Queries](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08419)" (Wen et al., 2023) arXiv:2310.08419 |
| System Prompt Extraction | System Prompt Extraction is an attack that involves exploiting vulnerabilities in AI models to retrieve internal instructions, known as system prompts, which guide the model's behavior. This type of attack targets models like GPT-4 by utilizing crafted queries or dialogues that can effectively leak these prompts. The implications of such an attack include significant risks to privacy and security, as it enables adversaries to manipulate AI outputs based on the extracted instructions. For example, the research has shown that simple text-based attacks can successfully reveal a model's system prompts with high probability. Methods such as the PLeak algorithm have been documented, emphasizing the challenge in defending against these attacks due to their stealthy nature. | "[Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805)" (Carlini et al., 2021) arXiv:2012.07805 |
| Side-Channel Attacks | Side-channel attacks are security exploits that gather information through indirect means, such as measuring physical emissions (power consumption or electromagnetic emissions) from cryptographic devices. These attacks do not target the underlying algorithm but the physical implementation of the system, allowing attackers to infer sensitive information like cryptographic keys. For instance, side-channel attacks include timing attacks and power analysis attacks that leverage the physical properties of a device to recover data inadvertently leaked during operations. They pose significant risks, especially in contexts involving AI and cryptographic systems. | "[Cache Telepathy: Leveraging Shared Resource Attacks to Learn DNN Architectures](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec20-yan.pdf)" (Yan et al., 2020) |
| Federated Learning Attacks | Federated Learning Attacks target the vulnerabilities inherent in federated learning frameworks, where multiple decentralized clients can influence a shared model without directly sharing their data. Attackers exploit this by manipulating local updates sent to a central server. Common approaches include model poisoning, where an attacker submits corrupted updates aiming to misclassify certain labels, and data poisoning, which alters the training data to increase test errors. A notable attack strategy includes the usage of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to produce benign-looking updates that are actually harmful. These mechanisms highlight the need for robust defense strategies to maintain the integrity and privacy in federated learning systems. | "[Federated Learning Privacy: Attacks, Defenses, Applications, and Policy Landscape - A Survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.03636)" (Zhao et al., 2024)<br/>[How To Backdoor Federated Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00459)" (Bagdasaryan et al., 2020) arXiv:1807.00459 |
| Privacy Attacks | Privacy attacks in AI systems refer to malicious techniques aimed at exploiting vulnerabilities to compromise individuals' data. These attacks target sensitive information through several techniques including data reconstruction, membership inference, model extraction, and property inference. For example, data reconstruction can reverse engineer private user information from aggregate data, while membership inference can reveal whether specific records were included in the training set. Such attacks raise significant concerns around data privacy and security, particularly as AI systems become more prevalent in various applications | "[A Survey of Privacy Attacks in Machine Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07646)", (Rigaki, Garcia, 2023)<br/>"[Privacy Attacks in Decentralized Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10001)", (El Mrini et al., 2024)<br/>"[Privacy in Large Language Models: Attacks, Defenses and Future Directions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10383)", (Li et al., 2023) |
